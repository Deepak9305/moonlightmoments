Decoding Alien Skies: How We Read Exoplanet Atmospheres

For a long time, just catching a glimpse — a faint, fleeting shadow — of a planet orbiting some distant star was enough to make headlines. Enough to feel like magic. But that bar has moved. Dramatically. We're not just spotting exoplanets now. We're reading their skies. Sniffing their air. Mapping what's floating in atmospheres hundreds of trillions of miles away, and asking whether any of it looks familiar — whether any of it looks like us.

Researchers study exoplanet atmospheres by analyzing light collected by space- and ground-based observatories. NASA's Hubble and James Webb Space Telescopes have measured chemical signatures in some of these distant worlds. Here is how transit spectroscopy works and what those early measurements can - and cannot - tell us.

How Do We See Invisible Air? The Power of Transit Spectroscopy

You can't just aim a telescope at an exoplanet and photograph its clouds. The star it orbits is blinding — everything else just drowns in that glare. So astronomers got creative. Brilliantly, almost sneakily creative. They use a technique called transit spectroscopy, and once you understand it, it's hard not to feel a little awestruck.

When a planet drifts across the face of its star — a transit — a thin sliver of that starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere on its way to us. Different gases intercept different wavelengths. Hydrogen absorbs here. Methane absorbs there. Carbon dioxide takes its slice. What arrives at our telescopes is starlight with gaps in it — specific colors missing, specific fingerprints left behind. Scientists analyze those spectral features to infer which gases may be present and estimate their abundance; results depend on models and follow-up observations.

The James Webb Era: Ripping Up the Textbooks

Hubble demonstrated that transmission spectroscopy could reveal clues about an exoplanet atmosphere. The James Webb Space Telescope extends that work into infrared wavelengths, where it can measure molecular signatures in some suitable targets. These measurements are sensitive to clouds, stellar activity and instrument noise, so they are interpreted with models rather than treated as direct photographs of an atmosphere.

  • WASP-39b: JWST made history here — the first time anyone had ever pulled a clear, unambiguous detection of carbon dioxide from an exoplanet's atmosphere. A line crossed that can't be uncrossed.
  • Escaping atmospheres: Hubble detected helium escaping from the atmosphere of WASP-107 b. Such observations help researchers study how close-in planets lose atmospheric gas over time.

The Ultimate Prize: Hunting for the Fingerprints of Life

A biosignature is a measurement that could be associated with life, but no single molecule is proof of biology. Scientists must test whether non-biological chemistry, geology, or measurement limits could explain the same signal, then seek independent evidence.

On Earth, life replenishes gases such as oxygen and methane, which react with each other. Finding both in another atmosphere could motivate further study, but it would not prove life: non-biological chemistry and geological processes can also produce potential biosignature gases. Researchers need multiple independent lines of evidence before drawing conclusions.

What has Webb found? JWST observations have detected methane and carbon dioxide in K2-18 b. Some analyses have proposed possible sulfur-bearing molecules, but their interpretation remains debated. No detection has established that life exists there.

What Comes Next in the Dark

The European Southern Observatory's Extremely Large Telescope is under construction in Chile and will have a 39-meter primary mirror. Its instruments are designed to advance the study of exoplanets and their atmospheres. Which worlds can be characterized will depend on the target, instrument, and observing conditions.

Every transit observed, every barcode decoded, chips away at the dark a little more. The question has already shifted from "are there other planets?" — we know that answer, there are billions — to something harder, quieter, and infinitely more loaded. We're not there yet. But we're closer than we've ever been.
